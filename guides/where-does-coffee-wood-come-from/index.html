Where Does Coffee Wood Come From? Origin, Season and Traceability

By Sarah · VietPaw · Updated 2 September 2026

Short answer: our coffee wood is the stem of Robusta coffee plants aged 20 years and over, grown on smallholder plots in Vietnam's Central Highlands — principally Dak Lak and Gia Lai. Growers remove trees that have stopped yielding economically as part of normal replanting; the stems, which previously went to firewood or were burned off in the field, are cut to billets of roughly 50–70 cm in the field, held about a year in ventilated shade, then machined, dried below 14% moisture and graded at our factory.

The plant, not a forest

Coffee wood is the woody stem of Coffea canephora — Robusta — which is the variety that dominates Vietnam's Central Highlands. It is an agricultural crop on a replanting cycle, not a timber species on a felling rotation. A plot is replanted when yield per tree no longer pays for the land and labour, usually after two decades or more, and the stems that come out are the raw material for these chews.

That distinction decides what can honestly be said about the product. No forest is cleared for it, nobody fells a tree that is still yielding cherries, and the material already existed with a low-value use. It also means coffee has never been through standardised timber testing, so there is no published density or Janka hardness figure for it — any number you see quoted is an estimate.

From plot to sealed carton

  1. Collection at the plantation edge
    Action: Stems from scattered smallholder plots are cut to billets and lifted by hand onto trailers.
    Result: Intake arrives across a collection season rather than in container loads.
  2. Seasoning
    Action: Billets are held about a year in ventilated shade.
    Result: Green stems that were heavy, dark and flexible become pale, light and hard.
  3. Cutting and shaping
    Action: Stems are cross-cut to the length band, bark removed, surface finished.
    Result: Sizes follow the natural stem and vary slightly piece to piece.
  4. Controlled drying
    Action: Shaped pieces are racked for the drying stages.
    Result: Finished moisture below 14%. Most rejection happens here — pieces that were going to crack crack on the rack.
  5. Grading and packing
    Action: Pieces are sorted into diameter bands, cracked pieces pulled, moisture measured and the carton marks checked against the packing list.
    Result: A sealed carton whose marks and moisture record match the paperwork.

Why availability follows the agricultural calendar

Supply is set by when growers replant, not by when orders arrive. Stems reach us from many scattered plots across a collection season, so a large single-size order placed outside that window waits on intake rather than on machine time. Plan a seasonal range on this material with the order placed before the season, not after a sales spike.

The same logic explains why diameter is quoted as a band. A size L is a piece that fell into the 3.5–4.5 cm band, not a piece machined to 4 mm tolerance. Two sticks of the same size will not weigh the same, because density varies stem to stem.

What a buyer can actually verify

Origin claims are worth only as much as the record behind them. For this material the verifiable items are the batch moisture reading, the grading rejection at drying, and the per-shipment plant-health documents — the Certificate of Origin, the phytosanitary certificate and the fumigation certificate, which are issued against the shipment and cannot be produced afterwards.

What does not exist, and should not be requested of any supplier in this category: FSC certification (coffee is an agricultural crop, outside its scope), a veterinary certificate (this is a wood article, not an animal by-product), a certified biodegradability claim for the finished toy, or a laboratory caffeine result unless you commission the test. Ask us if your market requires one.

Frequently asked

Where does coffee wood for dog chews come from?

From the stems of Robusta coffee plants aged 20 years and over, grown on smallholder plots in Vietnam's Central Highlands, principally Dak Lak and Gia Lai. The stems come out during the grower's normal replanting cycle.

Are trees cut down to make coffee wood chews?

No tree that is still yielding cherries is felled for this product, and no forest is cleared. The stems are removed by growers when the plant has reached the end of its economic yield, and previously went to firewood or were burned off in the field.

What part of the coffee plant is used?

The stem wood. Chew sticks are cut from the stem, not from the bean or the cherry. We do not publish a caffeine-free claim because no laboratory result has been produced for this material.

How long is the wood seasoned before it is made into chews?

About one year in ventilated shade as 50–70 cm billets, before cutting, shaping and the controlled drying stages that bring moisture below 14%. The seasoning is published; the drying parameters are not. What is published from that stage is the result on the finished batch, not the temperatures, timings or stage order.

Can I get FSC certification for coffee wood?

No. Coffee is an agricultural crop and falls outside the scope of FSC certification. The documents that do exist for this material are the Certificate of Origin, the phytosanitary certificate and the fumigation certificate.

Is the supply year-round?

Intake follows the replanting and collection season rather than arriving in steady container loads, so large single-size orders are best placed ahead of the season.
